Inside a data centre, chapter 11's gradient sync rides links of hundreds of gigabytes a second. Between data centres, or over ordinary networks, the link can be a thousand times slower, and sending the whole gradient every step stops being affordable. Below, four workers each compress their momentum DeMo's way, from the trained model, in your browser: each sends 4 numbers of every 64 and keeps the rest for later.

**This chapter is a toy, and says how far it is from the real thing.** The Decoder's
model has 5,376 parameters; here four simulated workers in one process each
train it on four of the default run's sixteen windows per step, for 2,000
steps, on one book, on a CPU, three seeds per setting. The
[Compute in Transit results site](https://compute-in-transit-results.vercel.app)
measured the same ideas on a 15.8-million-parameter GPT trained by eight
simulated workers for 3,000 steps on TinyStories, on GPUs, and then simulated
data-centre-scale links with those measurements. Its numbers, not these, say
what works at scale; this chapter explains the mechanisms its pages rely on and
shows them running on a model small enough to follow.

<span id="t-wan" />
**WAN and inter-data-centre bandwidth.** Inside a server, NVLink moves hundreds
of gigabytes a second per GPU; across a data centre's network, tens (chapter
11). A wide-area network (WAN) link between data centres is shared by a whole
site and can offer each training job far less: a 100 GbE link is 12.5 GB/s for
everything that crosses it. At those rates chapter 11's sum, step time equals
compute plus communication, is dominated by the communication: LLaMA 7B's dense
BF16 gradient (<R k="systems.collectives.bytes" div={1e9} d={1} /> GB) takes
over a second to send at 12.5 GB/s, every step, before any other job shares the
link. Training over slow links means sending less, sending less often, or both.

<span id="t-top-k" />
**Top-k sparsification.** Send only the $k$ largest values of a vector (and
where they are), here 1 in 16 per tensor. Most of a gradient's coordinates are
small at any one step, so most of the step survives. But each kept value needs
its position too: for the Decoder's model 2 bytes of BF16 value and 2 bytes of
position, so 1 value in 16 costs 4/32 of the dense bytes, not 1/16. Deep
Gradient Compression ([Lin et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01887)) does
this to an accumulated momentum: each worker adds its gradient into a local
momentum and an accumulator, sends the top 1/16 of the accumulator (that is
<R k="slowLinks.rows.1.bytes.payload" sep /> bytes a step here, against
<R k="slowLinks.rows.0.bytes.payload" sep /> for the dense gradient), and plain
SGD steps with the average of what was sent.

<span id="t-error-feedback" />
**Error feedback (the residual).** What a compressor drops is not thrown away:
each worker keeps it, the _residual_, and adds it to the next step's vector
before compressing again. A coordinate too small to be sent this step keeps
growing until it is large enough to be sent, so every update arrives eventually,
only late. Without this, the steady small coordinates of the gradient would
never be applied at all; with it, compressed SGD provably converges at the
uncompressed rate in the long run (Stich et al. 2018, ["sparsified SGD with
memory"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07599); Karimireddy et al. 2019, ["error
feedback fixes signSGD"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.09847)). 1-bit SGD ([Seide
et al.
2014](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2014/seide14_interspeech.html))
had used the same trick to send one bit per gradient value. DeMo's momentum _is_
its residual: the animation's second sync starts from a momentum that still
holds what the first did not send.

<span id="t-compressed-allgather" />
**Why compressed vectors are all-gathered, not all-reduced.** A ring all-reduce
(chapter 11) adds the workers' chunks as they pass, which needs every worker's
vector to have the same layout. Top-k vectors don't: each worker picks its own
positions (in the animation's chunk, worker 1 sends positions that worker 2 may
not). So the workers all-gather instead: every worker receives every other
worker's values and positions and adds them itself. A ring all-gather has each
worker send its own payload and forward the other $(p-2)$, so a worker's bytes
on the wire grow with the number of workers: with four workers DeMo's
<R k="slowLinks.rows.2.bytes.payload" sep /> bytes of payload become
<R k="slowLinks.rows.2.bytes.wire" sep /> on the wire, against the dense
all-reduce's <R k="slowLinks.rows.0.bytes.wire" sep />. With eight workers the
same payload costs 7 times itself, which is why the results site quotes both a
payload ratio and a smaller wire ratio.

<span id="t-demo" />
**DeMo (decoupled momentum).** [Peng et al.
(2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19870) let each worker keep its own momentum
instead of syncing gradients (Algorithm 1): fold the gradient into it, cut each
tensor into chunks, take each chunk's discrete cosine transform (DCT), send the
$k$ largest coefficients per chunk and subtract them from the momentum, so the
rest stays behind as error feedback. The DCT rearranges a chunk into frequency
components without losing anything (it is orthonormal: what is sent and what is
kept add up to the whole, checked in the frame tests), and the momentum's large,
slowly changing components tend to collect in a few coefficients, the "fast
components" that get sent. Everyone then averages the coefficients gathered,
transforms back, and steps by the _sign_ of the result. Here chunks are up to 64
values, $k =$ 4 of them, values in BF16 and positions in one byte:
<R k="slowLinks.rows.2.bytes.payload" sep /> bytes a step per worker.

<span id="t-diloco" />
**DiLoCo: syncing every H steps.** The other way to save bytes is to sync less
often. In DiLoCo ([Douillard et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08105),
Algorithm 1) each worker trains alone with AdamW for $H$ steps; then the workers
average how far they moved (the "pseudo-gradient") and an outer optimiser,
Nesterov momentum ([chapter 5](/training/05-optimisers#t-nesterov); learning
rate 0.7, momentum 0.9, the paper's setting), moves the shared weights; then
everyone starts the next $H$ steps from them. It sends the dense vector, but
only once per $H$ steps:
<R k="slowLinks.rows.3.bytes.wire" d={0} /> bytes a step on average at $H = 10$,
<R k="slowLinks.rows.4.bytes.wire" d={0} /> at $H = 50$. Local SGD, its
ancestor, is analysed by [Stich (2018)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09767).

**Measured on the toy.** Each method at the best of three learning rates
(chapter 6: a sign step, a plain SGD step and Adam's normalised step want very

(lr <R k="slowLinks.rows.0.bestLr" />
); top-k with error feedback <R k="slowLinks.rows.1.mean" d={3} /> ± <R k="slowLinks.rows.1.sd" d={3} />

(lr <R k="slowLinks.rows.2.bestLr" />, the top of its grid, so it might do
better higher still); DiLoCo <R k="slowLinks.rows.3.mean" d={3} /> ± <R k="slowLinks.rows.3.sd" d={3} /> at $H = 10$ and <R k="slowLinks.rows.4.mean" d={3} /> ± <R k="slowLinks.rows.4.sd" d={3} /> at
$H = 50$. Steps to a validation loss of <R k="slowLinks.target" />: dense <R k="slowLinks.rows.0.meanStepsToTarget" d={0} />, top-k <R k="slowLinks.rows.1.meanStepsToTarget" d={0} />, DeMo <R k="slowLinks.rows.2.meanStepsToTarget" d={0} /> (means over seeds); DiLoCo
did not get there in every seed. On this toy the compressors leave chapter 1's
plateau sooner than dense AdamW and DeMo ends lowest, while DiLoCo, whose
workers each see only 4 windows a step between syncs, lags. **Do not read that
order as general**: on the results site's GPT, dense AdamW ended lowest and
DeMo's top-k some way behind it. A 5,376-parameter character model on one book
is a different optimisation problem, and the compressors' big early steps
(DeMo's are a full learning rate on every weight) suit its plateau.

<span id="t-queueing" />
**Queueing on a shared link, and stragglers.** The step-time model assumes the
link is the job's alone. A shared WAN carries other traffic, and a sync that
arrives while the link is busy waits its turn. If other jobs' transfers arrive
at random and the link is busy a fraction $\rho$ of the time, a newcomer waits
on average $\rho/(2(1 - \rho))$ transfer-times for an M/D/1 queue (the
Pollaczek–Khinchine formula): <R k="systems.queue.0.formula" d={2} /> at 30%
load, <R k="systems.queue.2.formula" d={1} /> at 90% (a seeded simulation gives
<R k="systems.queue.0.simulated" d={2} /> and
<R k="systems.queue.2.simulated" d={1} />; results.md §11d). The wait grows
without bound as the link fills, and it hurts most the methods that send most.
Synchronous training has a second waiting problem: every step waits for the
slowest worker, a _straggler_. With eight workers whose step times vary by a
random 10% on average, the mean worker takes
<R k="systems.stragglers.0.meanWorker" d={3} /> time units a step but
synchronised steps take <R k="systems.stragglers.0.syncEveryStep" d={3} />;
syncing every 10 steps, as DiLoCo does, waits for the slowest _sum_ of 10 steps
instead: <R k="systems.stragglers.1.syncEveryH" d={3} /> per step (and
<R k="systems.stragglers.2.syncEveryH" d={3} /> every 50). The results site's
transit page shows the queueing effect on its recorded simulation runs.

**The hardware underneath.** Inside a data centre the slower links are the
scale-out network's: [Racks, chapter 5](https://ai-racks-explained.vercel.app/learn/05-scale-out-network) builds
its leaf, spine and core tiers and their oversubscription, and [chapter
9](https://ai-racks-explained.vercel.app/learn/09-training-clusters) shows which of a job's traffic crosses
them (the Racks site, in the Hardware track).

<Layer kind="maths">

**Top-k with error feedback** (DGC's form, per worker $i$, momentum $\mu$,
compressor $C$ = keep the top $k$ of each tensor):

$$
u_i \leftarrow \mu u_i + g_i, \quad v_i \leftarrow v_i + u_i, \quad s_i = C(v_i), \quad v_i \leftarrow v_i - s_i, \quad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \,\tfrac{1}{W}\textstyle\sum_i s_i
$$

(and $u_i$ is zeroed where $s_i$ was sent).

**DiLoCo**, every $H$ inner steps, with the outer Nesterov momentum $b$:

$$
\Delta = \tfrac{1}{W}\textstyle\sum_i \big(\theta - \theta_i\big), \quad b \leftarrow \mu b + \Delta, \quad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta_{\text{out}}(\Delta + \mu b)
$$

**Bytes on the wire per worker per step**, $W$ workers, $P$ parameters, 2-byte
values, payload $c$ bytes for a compressor:

$$
\text{dense: } 2\,\tfrac{W-1}{W}\,2P, \qquad \text{compressed (all-gather): } (W - 1)\,c, \qquad \text{DiLoCo: } 2\,\tfrac{W-1}{W}\,2P \,/\, H
$$

**M/D/1 waiting time** for a load $\rho$ of transfers each taking $s$:
$W_q = \rho\,s / (2(1 - \rho))$.

<Layer kind="code">

DeMo's compression of one chunk on one worker, from `demoSync` in
`src/lib/training/slowlinks.ts` (a port of `scripts/training/slowlinks.py`,
matched to its fixtures):

```ts
const ch = mom.slice(c * m, (c + 1) * m);
const coef = dctForward(ch, D);
const idx = topkIdx(coef, k);
const q = new Array<number>(m).fill(0);
const sent = idx.map((i) => coef[i]!);
idx.forEach((i, j) => {
  q[i] = sent[j]!;
  total[c]![i] = total[c]![i]! + sent[j]!;
  count[c]![i] = count[c]![i]! + 1;
});
const back = dctInverse(q, D);
for (let i = 0; i < m; i++) resid[c * m + i] = ch[i]! - back[i]!;
```

<Remember>

- Over slow links, send less (top-k, DeMo), less often (DiLoCo), or both.
- Error feedback keeps what was not sent and adds it to the next step, so every update arrives eventually.
- Compressed vectors are all-gathered, so their wire bytes grow with the number of workers.
- Compare methods at their own best learning rates, by the steps or time to a target loss.
